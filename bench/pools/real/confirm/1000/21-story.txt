THE SECOND SHOE

Wren Alderidge had been told three times that the shoes were wrong, and each time by a different person who believed they were the first to say so.

The Marlowe Street School of Ballet occupied the first floor above a shop that sold wool. The building shook gently whenever a lorry went by, and the mirrors at the far end of the big studio had a slight lean, so that the girls on the left of the barre looked taller than the girls on the right. Nobody had ever fixed this. It was said that the mirrors had been hung by a man who was in love with the piano teacher and had been looking at her rather than the level.

On the Thursday before the audition, Wren sat on the bench in the changing room with her pointe shoes in her lap, turning them over. She was fifteen, and she had been dancing in these for six weeks. The right one had gone soft in the box already. The left was perfect. This was not how it usually went; usually you broke in a pair evenly, like walking two dogs.

"You've sewn the ribbons on wrong," said Delphine, who was seventeen and who said everything as if reading it from a card. "Too far back. It pulls your heel."

"Madame Voss checked them."

"Madame Voss checks everybody's. She doesn't look."

Wren said nothing. The truth was that the left shoe was not hers. She had found it in the lost property box under the stairs in September, a single satin shoe, a size and a half too big, with a faded name inside the heel in blue ink that read ILSE. She had taken it because her own left had split the day after the new term began and her mother had said, with her eyes on the electric bill, that the new pair could wait until Christmas. Wren had darned the toe, packed it with lambswool, and told no one.

Now the audition was in nine days. The Ferncliffe Summer Intensive took twelve girls from a hundred and forty applicants, and its letter had said in a friendly way that candidates should wear well-fitted shoes in good condition.

At six o'clock the studio emptied. Wren stayed behind and stood in the middle of the floor, in the slightly leaning light, and tried a rise on the left foot alone. The shoe held. She did it again. The old satin creaked, and she had the odd feeling, which she would think about for years afterwards, that something in the shoe was waiting for her to do it properly.

She was still there when Madame Voss came in for her umbrella. Madame Voss was seventy and moved around the studio like someone checking that it still belonged to her. She stopped at the door and watched for a full minute without speaking, which Wren knew from experience was either very good or very bad.

"Whose is that shoe?" said Madame Voss at last.

"Mine, Madame."

"Don't be tiresome. The other one."

Wren took a breath and looked at the floor. "It was in the lost property. I only borrowed it. I was going to put it back."

"Ilse," said Madame Voss, and took her umbrella from the hook. "She danced here in 1974. She had a bunion the size of an egg and she would not hear a word about it. She left in a hurry, to marry a man in the navy, and she wrote to me every Christmas for thirty years. She always said she would come back for her shoes." She turned the umbrella over once. "Practise the combination from the second diagonal. Eight times, then go home."

Wren did the combination eight times. When she looked up the door was closed, and on the bench by the wall sat a paper bag she had not noticed before, with her name written on it in blue ink.
