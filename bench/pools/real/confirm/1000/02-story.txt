**Night Shift at Gate Nine**

Marit Solheim had worked the ramp at Halden Ground Services for eleven years, and she still could not sleep on the days after a night shift. At five in the morning the apron lights turned the wet concrete the colour of weak tea, and the first long-haul arrival of the day came in low over the fjord with its landing lights on, as if it were embarrassed to be early.

She stood beside the pushback tug with her headset around her neck and watched it taxi. Behind her, Tobias was eating a cheese roll in the cab and not talking, which was how she knew he was still angry about the schedule.

"Eighty-one minutes," he said finally, through the open window. "They gave us eighty-one minutes for a wide-body with a full cargo hold. Whoever wrote the roster has never lifted a pallet."

"Whoever wrote the roster is Henrik," Marit said. "He lifted plenty of pallets. That's why he hates us."

Tobias laughed, which was the end of the argument. It always ended that way, and she was grateful for it.

The aircraft stopped on the stand. The engines wound down, the chocks went in, and the long line of vehicles that had been waiting at the edge of the apron came forward like animals to a water hole: the belt loader, the fuel bowser, the catering lorry with its raised box, the two baggage trains. Marit counted them by habit and walked toward the forward hold.

Inside, the cargo door supervisor, a young woman named Ines Brandt who had joined in April, was holding a clipboard against her chest. She had the look of someone who had already checked three times and was about to check a fourth.

"Something's wrong with the manifest," Ines said. "Pallet fourteen is on the load sheet. It isn't in the hold."

"Maybe it was offloaded at the first stop."

"Then it would say so. It says it's here. Forty kilos of something, cleared by customs in Frankfurt, signed for by a company called Lindqvist Marine Instruments." She turned the clipboard around. "I can't find it. And the form number has been written over."

Marit looked at the sheet. Someone had gone over a seven with a pen until it became a one, or a one until it became a seven, and the ink was a different blue from the rest.

"Don't touch it," she said. "Photograph it. Then go and find Henrik, and don't let him tell you it's a printing error."

Ines went. Marit stayed by the open door, in the cold smell of metal and rubber, and listened to the belt loader begin its low whine. Somewhere below her a driver was singing, badly, an old song about a boat.

She had seen missing cargo before. Mostly it was a mistake, a pallet in the wrong cage or a label that fell off in the rain. But a rewritten form number was not a mistake, and she thought about it all through the turnaround, while the bags came up the belt and the catering lorry lowered its box and Tobias shouted a joke across the apron that she did not hear.

At six-fifty the aircraft pushed back. Marit signed the turnaround sheet, and in the box marked "irregularities" she wrote one line, in careful capitals, and did not yet know that she had just started the longest week of her working life.
