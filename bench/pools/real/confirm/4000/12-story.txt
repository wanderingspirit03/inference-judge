These are the first three chapters of a short story I am writing. Please continue it at length in the same voice, with at least two more chapters, and bring it to an ending that feels earned. Keep the quiet, slightly dry tone, the present-tense narration close to Ines, and the details about how the mountain actually works.

# The Night Lift

## 1.

At ten past five in the morning the snow is still the colour of slate, and Ines Marchetti is already standing at the bottom station of the Kestrel chair with a thermos in one hand and a wrench in the other, listening to the cable. She has done this every working morning for eleven winters. The sound she is waiting for is not a sound exactly. It is the absence of one particular note, a thin sort of whine that the main sheave makes when the bearing is going, and which she has never once been able to describe to anyone who has not heard it.

Today there is no whine. There is only the long low breath of the motor turning over with nobody on the line, and below it, from the car park, the first diesel of the day: Gustavo, the groomer, taking his machine up the Gentian run to finish the corduroy before the lifts open.

She puts the wrench down on the rail, unscrews the thermos, and drinks. The coffee is too sweet. Her daughter filled it last night as a favour and has never learned that Ines takes it black. She drinks it anyway.

The resort of Kestrel Hollow has four lifts, nineteen runs, a hotel with a name nobody uses, and a population of two hundred and six in winter, which swells to something over four thousand on the weekends if the snow report is kind. It sits in a bowl of rock that the guidebooks call "sheltered" and the locals call "the sink," because everything that falls on the surrounding peaks eventually comes down to it, whether it is snow, or weather, or rumour. Ines grew up three valleys east and came here at twenty-six for a season, to get away from a man, and stayed because the lift needed someone who would not skip the morning check.

Her daughter, Lucía, is sixteen and sleeps through all of this. She lives in the small flat above the ski store with the window that looks out on the Hollow, and she has her own opinions about the mountain, most of which are unfavourable. She wants to go to the city for school and then, she says, never come back. Ines has not told her that this is exactly what she once said herself, in a kitchen three valleys east, to a mother who had said nothing at all and simply poured the tea.

By six she has walked the whole length of the bottom terminal. She has checked the safety gate, the emergency stop, the loading carpet, the grip tester, and the little brass plaque with the name of the engineer who commissioned the lift, a man who died before she was born and whom she thinks of, without any sentiment, as a colleague. She signs the log. The log says, in her handwriting, in the column where other people write "nothing to report," *Nothing to report.* She has never been able to bring herself to write it any other way, even on the mornings when it is not quite true.

At seven the others arrive. Teodor comes first, nineteen and long-limbed, with the lanyard of the seasonal staff still new enough to be stiff. Then Marguerite from the ticket office, who tells Ines every morning, in the same words, that the roads were awful, and then Dag, the patrol leader, who says nothing and goes straight to the radio.

"Wind at the top?" Ines asks him.

"Twelve. Gusting twenty-five." He does not look up from the handset. "We open the upper section at nine if it holds."

"It won't hold."

"Then we open at ten," says Dag, and that is the whole of the morning meeting.

## 2.

The first thing that goes wrong is small. At a quarter to nine, with the lower section already running and a line of about forty people shuffling toward the carpet in their bright jackets, the third chair from the end of the loading zone sticks on its grip. It does not fall. It does not even slow. It simply fails to release the cable cleanly, so that for the length of one breath the whole carrier tilts, a small grey animal shrugging its shoulder, and then it rights itself and goes on up the hill with two teenagers in it who have not noticed anything at all.

Ines notices. She is already moving before she has decided to, down the loading ramp, one hand on the stop button and her eyes on the cable. She does not press it. She has pressed it exactly four times in eleven years, and each time she has been right, and each time she has spent the following week explaining herself to the insurers. She watches the next chair come around and puts her hand on the grip as it passes, as a doctor lays two fingers on a wrist, and feels it: a drag, a stickiness, like a drawer that has swollen in damp weather.

"Teodor," she says. "Take the carpet."

"I've only done it once on my own."

"Then this is the second time."

She goes into the machine room and finds the grease gun, and then, because she is the kind of person who checks the thing she does not want to be true, she goes back out and counts the chairs. There are seventy-two on the line. She walks the length of the lower station and puts a hand on the grip of every seventh one as it comes by. By the time she has done ten she has found three that drag.

It is the grease, she thinks. A batch of it from the new supplier, which Per in the workshop swore was the same specification as the old, and which she had signed for without testing, for the first time in her life, because it was the last week of November and everyone was tired.

She radios Dag. "I'm going to close the Kestrel for an hour. Maybe two."

There is a silence on the channel that she knows well. It is the silence of a man doing arithmetic with money. The Kestrel is the only chair that reaches the top terminal, and the top terminal is where the hotel's guests are waiting for the one run that matters, the long Gentian, which today has the best snow in the valley.

"Do you need to?" says Dag finally.

"I think so."

"Think, or know?"

"Dag," she says. "If you want me to know, I will have to let one fail."

Another silence. Then: "Close it. I'll tell reception."

She presses the stop button. The line shudders and halts, forty chairs hanging in the cold, and from the queue at the bottom comes a long ragged groan, the sound of a crowd that has just been told something it has already guessed. She does not turn around to look at them.

While they are working, Valentina Brask comes down from the hotel. She is the manager, a tall woman in a long camel coat who has run the place for four winters and still wears city shoes on the mountain, and she walks along the cordon tape with the unhurried air of someone who has already decided what she is going to say.

"I have sixty guests who booked the week for the Gentian," she says. "Two of them are on the phone to their travel agent as we speak."

"I know."

"Is it something you can fix?"

"It's something I am fixing," says Ines, and keeps her eyes on the grip she is cleaning. "If you want it quicker, there is no way to make it quicker. If you want it safe, this is the way."

Valentina is quiet for a moment. The wind lifts the hem of her coat and lets it fall. "The last manager," she says, "would have had you running by ten, on the grounds that the chairs were going round anyway."

"The last manager was a fool."

"Yes," says Valentina. "That is more or less why he is the last one." She looks at the line of grey chairs, the bright cold air, the mountain going up behind them into cloud, and something in her shoulders gives way, only a little. "Tell me when it is ready. I will tell the guests it is a precaution. It will be a precaution, won't it?"

"It is a precaution today," says Ines.

Valentina catches the word that has not been said, the *today*, and takes it back up the hill with her, and does not mention it again.

It takes her and Per and Teodor an hour and fifty minutes to flush and re-grease the grips on every carrier, working down the line in the lee of the terminal with the wind pulling at their sleeves. The grease from the new supplier is the colour of butter, and when she rubs a little of it between her fingers it feels wrong, a fine grit under the smoothness, like sugar stirred into a paste. She puts a sample in a jar and writes the date on the lid.

At eleven fifty the lift is running again, and by noon the line has doubled. Nobody thanks her. A man in a red jacket mutters something about a "pointless delay" to his wife as he passes, and Ines, who has heard worse in three languages, lifts her thermos to him and smiles.

## 3.

That evening, after the last skier has been swept off the Gentian and the lights along the lower slope have gone out one by one, Ines is still in the machine room with the jar on the bench in front of her. The room smells of oil and hot metal and the faint sweetness of the coffee she has not finished. Outside, the wind has finally dropped. She can hear, through the thin wall, the tick of the cooling motor.

She has a phone number for the supplier and a mobile number for their sales representative, a young man called Ruben Aalto who visited in October with a box of free gloves and a very good-looking brochure. She calls the first and gets a recorded message in a language that is not quite the one she is expecting. She calls the second and he answers on the sixth ring, cheerful, with the noise of a restaurant behind him.

"Ines! How is the mountain?"

"There is grit in your grease."

A pause. The restaurant noise recedes, as though he has walked into a corridor. "Grit," he says.

"I have it in a jar. I can send you a photo. Three of the carriers were dragging this morning, and I closed my lift for two hours on a Saturday with a full house."

"That is, I mean, I am really sorry about that. But those batches are all tested, I can assure you."

"I'm sure they are," says Ines. "I'm also sure that I am looking at them."

When the call ends she sits for a while with her elbows on the bench. It is not a disaster, she knows that. A grip that drags can be cleaned. A chair that tilts for a breath is not a chair that falls. But she has spent eleven winters learning to tell the difference between a thing that has gone wrong and a thing that is about to, and the part of her that does this has been awake since a quarter to nine, saying very quietly: this is the second.

The first was in March, though she had not counted it then. A bolt on the upper station, an inspection hatch that someone had closed without the second latch. She had found it at six in the morning and fixed it in a minute and written *Nothing to report* in the log. She had not wanted a fuss. She had not wanted to be the woman who made the engineers drive up from the city for a latch.

She takes the log from the shelf and opens it to March, and there, under the fourteenth, is her own handwriting, neat and wrong. She reads it three times. Then she turns to today's page, and for the first time in eleven years, in the column where she writes the sentence she is proudest of, she writes something else.

*Grips dragging on 11 carriers. Lift closed 08:50 to 11:50. Grease from new supplier has grit in it; sample retained in the workshop, labelled. Also: inspection hatch, upper station, 14 March, second latch missing. Should have reported. I am reporting it now.*

She looks at it for a long time. Then she signs it, and at the bottom, on a line of its own, she adds the time.

Per comes in as she is closing the log, smelling of the workshop, a stocky man of sixty with a cap he wears indoors and out. He puts a half-empty tin on the bench beside the jar, turns it so the label faces her, and taps the batch number with a thick finger.

"Four tins left in the store," he says. "Same batch. I have taped them shut."

"You didn't have to stay."

"I signed for the delivery too," says Per. "You are not the only one in this building who does not like the taste of being wrong." He studies the jar, then her face. "The old supplier is still in business. I can ring them in the morning."

"Ring them," says Ines. "And ask them what they charge to be right."

"They will say too much."

"It will still be cheaper than the alternative," she says, and Per, who has never in her hearing agreed with anything aloud, nods once and takes his cap off, as one does at a small funeral.

When she steps outside, Teodor is sitting on the steps of the terminal with his boots off and his socks steaming in the cold, looking up at the sky, which has cleared. There are more stars than she remembers from last winter. Behind him the Kestrel chairs hang motionless, a long string of them rising into the dark, and the snow beneath them has taken on a blue colour she has never found a word for in any of her three languages.

"Is it bad?" he says.

"No." She sits down next to him on the step, which is freezing through her trousers. "It's the kind of thing that is only bad if you pretend it isn't."

He thinks about this with the seriousness of nineteen. "Is that a rule?"

"It's about the only one I've got."

Down in the village a dog starts barking, and then another, and from somewhere behind the hotel she hears the engine of Gustavo's machine starting up for the night shift on the Gentian, its lights swinging across the slope like a slow lighthouse. Ines finishes the last of her coffee, which has gone cold and is still too sweet, and finds that she does not mind it.

"Tomorrow," she says, "you can do the carpet again. All morning."

"On my own?"

"With me standing right behind you," she says. "Which is how everyone starts."
