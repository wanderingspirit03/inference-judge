THE LAST WOODEN HORSE

1.

On the morning the order came, Odile Fennimore was sanding the left hind leg of a rocking horse and thinking about nothing at all, which was how she liked to begin the day.

The Pettibone Toy Works stood at the bottom of Cutler's Hill, where the road gave up and became a footpath to the canal. It had been a brewery once, and the smell had never quite left; on damp days the paint shop still gave off a note of old hops, and the older workers claimed that the glue held better because of it. There were forty-one people on the payroll in the spring, which was down from a hundred and ten when Odile started as a girl of sixteen, and in the long upstairs room where the horses were made there were now only three benches in use out of twelve. The other nine were covered with dust sheets, like furniture in a house that is being sold.

She heard Mr. Garrow's footsteps before she saw him. He walked with the heavy care of a man carrying something that might spill, and he was carrying nothing but a single sheet of paper.

"Odile," he said. "A moment."

She put down the sanding block and blew the dust off her wrists. At the next bench Tobias, who was nineteen and had been sent to them by the council's training scheme, stopped pretending to look at a joint.

"It's Harcourt and Vane," Mr. Garrow said. "They want six hundred."

"Six hundred what?"

"Horses. The big pattern, the dapple grey, with the real horsehair tail. For the autumn catalogue. They want them in the warehouse by the second of October."

Odile did the sum without meaning to, as she did all sums, with her lips slightly pressed. The shop could make eleven horses a week when nothing went wrong and the glue was kind. It was the ninth of August.

"That's seven weeks," she said. "Seventy-seven horses, if we are lucky."

"Yes."

"They know that."

"They do not know that," said Mr. Garrow. "They know that they ordered forty last year and we delivered forty. They have looked at a spreadsheet and seen that the number can be made bigger." He laid the paper on her bench, carefully, as though it too might spill. "They are offering a price that would pay for the roof."

Both of them looked up. There was a place above the paint shop where a bucket sat all winter on a plank, and neither of them mentioned it.

"And if we say no?"

"They go to the factory in the east. The one with the machines. And we do not get asked again."

Odile picked up the paper and read it twice, once for the numbers and once for the tone, which was friendly in the way of someone who has already decided. At the bottom was a name, Ms. Imre Calloway, Head of Seasonal Buying, and a line of small print saying that horses must be, quote, of consistent finish and delivered in carton packs of six.

"Consistent finish," she said.

"I know."

"Every horse I've made has a different face. That is the whole reason people want them."

"I know, Odile," said Mr. Garrow, and he looked, for a moment, very tired. "I'm only the man who brought it up the stairs."

2.

By lunchtime the whole building knew. News in the Pettibone Toy Works did not travel by gossip so much as by vibration; you could feel it pass through the floor, from the stairs to the packing room to the yard, where the delivery lorry driver put out his cigarette and asked what had happened.

They held the meeting in the canteen because it was the only room with enough chairs. Mr. Garrow stood by the tea urn. Beside him was his daughter Priya, who kept the books and who had come home from a job in the city three years ago with a quiet expression and a laptop that she guarded like a pet.

"I have done the figures," Priya said. "I will say them once, and then you can argue with them. If we take the order and we hit it, we are secure for two years. If we take it and we miss, Harcourt and Vane do not pay for anything late and the penalty clause is eight percent of the whole order. That is about the cost of two of the three benches that are still running."

"So we do not miss," said Bernard from the paint shop, who was sixty-seven and had a voice like gravel in a tin.

"We cannot make six hundred horses by hand in seven weeks, Bernard. We could not do it with twelve benches."

"Then we need machines," said Tobias, and everyone looked at him.

He went red but did not stop. "There's a router at the college. A numerical one. It cuts the body from a block in twenty minutes. I did a unit on it. The council course has a loan scheme for small firms and the college would lend us the machine for evenings."

There was a silence in which the urn ticked.

"A router does not make a horse," said Odile.

"No. But it makes the parts, and you could put them together. And paint them. And you do the faces."

"Consistent finish," said Bernard, and spat the words like a seed.

Odile did not answer at once. She was looking at the long windows along the canteen wall, where the light came in grey from the canal, and thinking about her father, who had carved the first of the dapple greys in 1961 from the trunk of a pear tree that had fallen in the vicarage garden. He had said that a horse was finished when it looked as though it was about to be let go. She had never been able to explain that to anyone who asked how long a horse took.

"Tell me what the college has," she said at last.

Tobias told her. It was a machine the size of a small car, and it had been used for chair legs and for the school's rocket club. It could not do the head, which had too many curves, but it could do the barrel and the four legs and the rocker, and it could cut them so that each fitted with a tongue and a groove without a drop of glue. Priya was writing as he spoke. Mr. Garrow had sat down.

"Two hundred and fifty horses from the machine, perhaps," Priya said when he had finished. "Plus our seventy-seven by hand. That is not six hundred."

"No," said Odile. "But it is more than I thought a minute ago."

She got up. Her knees ached from the stairs, as they always did in August, when the damp came off the canal.

"Mr. Garrow," she said. "Ring Ms. Calloway. Ask her what 'consistent' means to her. Ask her if she has ever held one."
