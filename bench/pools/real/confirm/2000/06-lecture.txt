**Module 6: Fluid therapy in dogs and cats (lecture notes, week 9)**

**1. Why we give fluids**
- Three aims: replace a deficit (dehydration), cover ongoing losses (vomiting, diarrhoea, polyuria), and meet daily maintenance needs.
- Fluids are not harmless. Too much, too fast causes overload, especially in cats and in animals with heart disease or kidney failure.

**2. Types of fluid**
- Crystalloids: isotonic (for example a balanced replacement solution or 0.9% saline) stay in the extracellular space; about 25% of the volume stays in the blood vessels after an hour.
- Hypotonic maintenance fluids: only for maintenance, never for resuscitation, because they carry little sodium.
- Colloids: large molecules that hold water inside vessels; used for low protein or shock under supervision of the vet.
- Hypertonic saline (7.2%): small volume, rapid effect, short lasting, must be followed by isotonic fluid.

**3. Assessing dehydration**
- Percentage is estimated from skin tent, gum moistness, eye position, heart rate and capillary refill time.
- Under 5%: not detectable. 5%: slightly tacky gums. 8%: skin tent persists, eyes slightly sunken. 10%: marked skin tent, dull and sunken eyes, fast pulse. 12% or more: shock, collapse.
- Weigh the animal. Acute weight loss is the best measure of fluid loss.

**4. Calculations**
- Maintenance rate: 50 ml/kg/day for dogs over 10 kg is a safe working figure; smaller dogs and cats are often given 60 ml/kg/day.
- Deficit (litres) = body weight (kg) x dehydration fraction. For example, a 20 kg dog 8% dehydrated has a deficit of 20 x 0.08 = 1.6 litres.
- Replace the deficit over 12 to 24 hours, not all at once, unless the animal is in shock.
- Total over 24 hours = deficit + maintenance + estimated ongoing losses.
- Drip rate: drops per minute = (volume in ml x drops per ml of the giving set) / (time in minutes). A standard set gives 20 drops per ml; a paediatric set gives 60 drops per ml.

**5. Shock doses**
- Dog: up to 90 ml/kg of crystalloid in the first hour, given in boluses of one quarter to one third and reassessing after each.
- Cat: up to 60 ml/kg, in smaller boluses of 10 to 15 ml/kg given over 15 minutes.
- Reassess after every bolus: heart rate, pulse quality, gum colour, temperature of paws, breathing.

**6. Routes**
- Intravenous is the route of choice. Cephalic vein first. Use a catheter that is as short and as wide as the patient allows.
- Subcutaneous: only for mild dehydration in stable animals; absorption is slow and unreliable if the animal is cold or in shock.
- Intraosseous: for neonates and collapsed small animals when no vein can be found.
- Oral: preferred if the animal is drinking and not vomiting.

**7. Monitoring**
- Check at least every four hours: body weight, urine output (target 1 to 2 ml/kg/hour), heart and breathing rate, mucous membranes, catheter site.
- Signs of overload: clear nasal discharge, chemosis (swollen conjunctiva), cough, noisy breathing, rapid weight gain. Stop the fluids and call the vet.

**8. Catheter care**
- Wash hands, wear gloves, clip and clean the site with the surgical scrub.
- Change the dressing every day and inspect for swelling, heat or pain; remove the catheter at the first sign of phlebitis.
- Label the giving set with the date and time it was started; change it every 72 hours.

**9. Special cases**
- Cats with heart disease or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: use about half the usual rate, small boluses of 5 ml/kg, and weigh twice a day. Rapid breathing at rest means stop.
- Kidney failure: fluids are the main treatment, but urine output must be measured with a closed collection system; if output falls below 1 ml/kg/hour after rehydration, tell the vet at once because the animal may be overloaded.
- Diabetic ketoacidosis: use saline first, add potassium once the vet has a blood result, and never use a fluid with glucose until the blood sugar has fallen.
- Puppies and kittens: use paediatric giving sets, warm all fluids, and measure the rate with a syringe pump, since a few millilitres matter.
- Blood loss: crystalloid alone dilutes the blood further. The vet may choose a blood transfusion; nurses prepare the cross-match and monitor for transfusion reactions (fever, tremor, vomiting, hives).

**10. Worked example from the lecture**
A 4 kg cat has been vomiting for three days and is judged to be 7% dehydrated. The deficit is 4 x 0.07 = 0.28 litres, which is 280 ml. Maintenance at 60 ml/kg/day is 240 ml. We replace the deficit over 12 hours, so 280 / 12 = 23 ml/hour. We also give maintenance over 24 hours, so 240 / 24 = 10 ml/hour. The starting rate is 23 + 10 = 33 ml/hour for the first 12 hours, then 10 ml/hour plus any ongoing losses measured during that time. If the cat vomits 30 ml in a four-hour period, we add 30 / 4 = 7.5 ml/hour to the rate.

**10A. Adding things to the bag**
- Potassium chloride is the commonest additive. Typical supplement is 20 mmol per litre when the blood potassium is 3.0 to 3.5 mmol/litre, and more only on the vet's instruction. The maximum safe rate is 0.5 mmol/kg/hour, so a 10 kg dog on a supplemented fluid at 20 mmol/litre must not run faster than 250 ml/hour. Always check this limit before setting the pump.
- Label the bag clearly with the additive, the amount, the date and your initials. A bag without a label is thrown away.
- Never add drugs to a bag of blood or to colloid. Never run calcium-containing fluid through the same line as blood.
- Glucose 5% in water is not a replacement fluid. It is mainly water once the sugar is used, so it can cause low sodium if given in large volumes.

**11. Revision checklist**
- Know the dehydration signs by percentage.
- Be able to do deficit, maintenance and drip-rate calculations without a calculator for simple weights.
- Know the signs of overload and the first action.
- Know which route suits which patient.
- Know what to record on the fluid chart.

**12. Common mistakes (from the case review session)**
- Calculating deficit but forgetting the maintenance part.
- Setting a pump in ml per hour when the order was in ml per kg per hour.
- Giving potassium-supplemented fluid faster than 0.5 mmol/kg/hour.
- Not warming fluids for small patients, so they become hypothermic.
