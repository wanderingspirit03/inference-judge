This is the start of a short story I have been working on for a while and I am stuck on what happens next. Could you continue it for me at length, something like the next two chapters, in the same quiet voice and in the same close third person? I want the discrepancy in the controlled drugs register to matter, but I do not want it to turn into a thriller.

THE NIGHT COUNT

1.

The pharmacy at Saint Ambrose Infirmary was in the basement, which the porters called the Cellar and the management called Level B, and which Marguerite Osei had thought of for eleven years simply as the place where her feet stopped hurting. The lift opened onto a corridor painted a green nobody would have chosen. At the end of it was a hatch with a sliding glass panel, and behind the hatch, shelves, and behind the shelves, a second room that few people at the hospital had ever seen, where the controlled drugs lived in a steel cupboard the size of a wardrobe.

She came on at nine in the evening, as she had done on Mondays, Tuesdays and Wednesdays for as long as the rota had allowed her to choose, and the first thing she did was what she always did. She took off her coat, hung it on the third hook, and stood for a moment with her hand on the cold edge of the dispensing bench. The building made a low sound at night, a long machine breath from the ventilation, and the sound told her that things were as they should be. When the sound changed, she noticed, in the way a person with a sleeping child notices a cough.

"You're early," said Tobias.

Tobias Lindqvist was the evening technician, twenty-six, thin, with a habit of reading the labels aloud under his breath while he worked, so that the room always seemed faintly to be conducting a service. He was sitting on the high stool with a tray of returns in front of him, sorting the unopened from the opened. Opened could not go back on the shelf. Opened went into the blue bin, and the blue bin went to be burned, and every month she looked at the cost of what went into it and felt something she had no word for that was neither anger nor sadness.

"Ten minutes," she said. "The buses were kind."

"Wards sent four urgents since six. Two are done. The oncology one is waiting for your check and the other is a discharge for Eleven, which I'd leave until the doctor stops changing his mind." He tapped a printed sheet with a pen. "And the fridge in the aseptic room is at nine point one. Not alarming. But it was seven this morning."

"Did you log it?"

"I logged it."

"Then I'll look at it at midnight, and if it's still climbing I'll call the engineers." She said it lightly. She was already moving toward the oncology prescription, which was waiting in its tray with its small yellow flag, and she felt, as she always did on these first minutes of a shift, the pleasure of an ordinary difficulty. A dose, a weight, a surface area, a sum that must come out the same way twice. There was no one in the world, she thought, who would thank her for getting a sum right. That was what made it a good job.

The prescription was for a woman called Pauline Ardley, aged seventy-one, a standard cycle of a drug Marguerite knew as well as she knew her own telephone number. She worked the figures on paper first, then on the calculator, then she handed the paper to Tobias and said, "Do it yourself without looking at mine," and he did, murmuring, and when the two numbers matched she initialled the sheet, and he initialled it, and the bag went into the clean room. It took eleven minutes. It would have taken three if she had trusted herself alone, and that was exactly why they did it the slow way.

At ten past ten the evening porter brought down the ward requests in a plastic crate, as he did every night, a broad quiet man named Dessie who had worked at the infirmary for thirty years and who had once, at a retirement party, been seen to weep openly at a speech about someone he had not liked. He put the crate on the bench and said, "Seven's got a visitor who fainted. Nothing to do with us. But she was in the corridor all the same, going on about the lift."

"Is she all right?"

"Sitting up, drinking tea. Sister Okafor has her."

"Then she's in the best hands in the building," said Marguerite, and Dessie nodded, because he agreed, and left.

At midnight she looked at the fridge. Eight point four. It had turned back by itself, which was the worst possible outcome, because it meant there was nothing to report and still something not understood. She wrote a line in the log anyway. Tobias had gone to the staff room for his break, and the pharmacy was quiet except for the breath of the ventilation, and for the first time that night she let herself think about the thing she had been avoiding thinking about since Sunday.

2.

On Sunday she had done the weekly reconciliation of the controlled drugs register, a job that fell to her because nobody else wanted it and because, she suspected, her colleagues believed that her kind of patience was a fixed property, like height. It was not hard. You took the book, which was a real book with numbered pages that nobody was allowed to tear, and you compared every entry to the stock in the steel cupboard, ampoule by ampoule and tablet by tablet, and where the book and the cupboard agreed you ticked, and where they did not agree you did not tick, and there was a form.

She had counted the morphine sulphate ten milligram ampoules three times. The book said forty-two. The cupboard held forty. She had put her glasses on, taken them off, checked the batch numbers, counted the empties in the returns tray, and checked the ward registers that Sister Okafor and the other ward managers kept, which had to match, and which did, line for line, down to the last dose given. Two ampoules had left the cupboard and had not been written down. Or two ampoules had been written down as being in the cupboard that had never been there at all. The difference between those two possibilities was the whole of the problem, and she could not find a way to tell them apart.

She filled in the form. She was not frightened, exactly. The form was a good form: it asked what had been checked, and by whom, and she had checked everything, and written it all down in a small upright hand that her mother had taught her, and she had signed it, and she had put it in the tray for the chief pharmacist, Dr. Halloran, whose name was on the door of a room upstairs that she had been inside only four times.

On Monday morning there had been no response, which was normal. On Tuesday, today's Tuesday, she had been on days at the clinic in the annex, and had not gone down to the Cellar at all, and had told herself that she was giving it time. In the afternoon, in the clinic, a patient had asked her whether anyone really checked the pharmacy, and she had said that yes, everything was checked twice, and had heard how bright her own voice sounded, and had felt like a person speaking through the glass hatch.

Now it was ten past midnight on Wednesday and she stood in the second room with the key in her hand. Every controlled drug was in a cupboard that could only be opened by two people, in principle. In practice, on the night shift, with one pharmacist and one technician and no one else, it was understood that the key would sit with the pharmacist and the technician would be called in as witness. She had always obeyed this. She obeyed it now and walked through to the staff room to get Tobias.

He was eating a sandwich in a way that suggested he had been told that he must. He put it down at once.

"Do I need to bring a pen?"

"Always bring a pen," she said, and he did.

She opened the cupboard. The steel door made the faint cough it always made, and the inside smelled of nothing, of cold metal and cardboard. Together they counted the morphine, and the number was forty, as it had been on Sunday, and both of them wrote it down and signed. She did not tell him why. She did not want him to count differently if he knew what he was supposed to find. This, too, was a rule, one she had made for herself years ago and had never had to explain.

"Forty," Tobias said. "Is it supposed to be forty?"

"It's supposed to be what it is."

He looked at her, with the particular expression of the young when they are working out how much of what an older person says is a lesson. "That's a very good answer," he said, "and I'd like to know what it means."

"It means that I would like you to go and sit down for ten minutes with your sandwich, and not ask me anything else tonight."

He went. She heard him pause in the doorway and decide against saying something, and she was grateful, and also faintly ashamed to be grateful, because it was his shift as well and he was entitled to know what was happening in the room he worked in.

3.

The morphine book had two hands in it. This was not strange in itself; the whole department wrote in it, over the years: the technician who left in the spring, the locum with the looping letters, herself, Tobias, and three or four others whose handwriting she knew better than their faces. But she took the book to the bench in the main dispensary and opened it at the page for the previous Thursday, and beside the entries for that day, in the narrow column where the pharmacist on duty entered the receipt of stock from the wholesaler, there was a line she did not recognise.

The delivery had been logged as fifty ampoules, received at 4:40 in the afternoon. She had been told by the wholesaler's paperwork, which was stapled into the back of the book, that the delivery was for fifty. The cupboard had held fifty-two, once those had been added, and so the book said forty-two on Sunday, after ten were used that week. But she had counted forty. So either the delivery was short, and someone had signed for fifty when only forty-eight had arrived, or the stock had been correct and two ampoules had left.

She turned the stapled paper over. The delivery note was signed with a flourish and a surname she could not read, and beside it a time: 4:40. Underneath, in a different pen, someone had written the initials H.B. She frowned. There was no H.B. in the department. There was, on the staff list, a Hamish Brennan, who worked in the stores, and who would, in the ordinary way, take in a delivery from the wholesaler and carry the boxes down to the Cellar and leave them at the hatch. But the controlled drugs did not come to the hatch. They came by hand to the pharmacist on duty, and on Thursday afternoon the pharmacist on duty had been Neil Castellan, who was good, and fast, and almost never without a coffee.

She thought about Neil. She did not think he had done anything. She could have said this to anyone, and it would have been a statement about character, which is not evidence, and which people in Dr. Halloran's position are trained to set aside. She thought about the morning when Neil had brought his daughter in for a school project, and the girl had asked, at the hatch, whether the drugs were kept in a safe, and he had said no, a safe is for money, this is more serious, and the girl had been quiet for a whole minute, taking it in.

At half past one, Tobias came back from his break and found her sitting with the book open and a pencil behind her ear that she had forgotten about.

"Is it something I did?" he asked.

"No."

"Because I'd rather you told me."

"It is nothing you did," she said. "I think it is an arithmetic mistake, and I think I will find it, and I would rather not say more until I have, because if I am wrong I will have said something to you about someone else that I should not have said."

He thought about this. "That's a very high standard," he said.

"Yes. It's inconvenient."

Upstairs, six floors above them, a bell went in the ward corridor and a nurse's shoes crossed the polished floor, and in the Cellar the ventilation changed its note, very slightly, and she heard it. She took the pencil from behind her ear, and she drew a small ruled line under the delivery entry, and wrote the date, and her initials, and the word *query*. Then she put the book in the drawer under the bench, locked it, put the key in her pocket, and went to see whether anyone on Ward Seven needed anything.

4.

Ward Seven was on the fourth floor, and she took the stairs, because the lift was the lift the visitor had gone on about, and because the stairs gave her four flights in which to put away the person she had been in the Cellar. Pharmacists were not expected on wards at that hour. The ward staff tolerated it the way a household tolerates a relative who arrives with a casserole.

Sister Okafor was at the nurses' station with the lights turned down to a shade the hospital called night mode and the nurses called the aquarium. She was a large, unhurried woman in her fifties with reading glasses on a cord, and she looked up at Marguerite and did not ask what she was doing there, which was one of the reasons Marguerite trusted her.

"I haven't ordered anything," said the Sister.

"No. I wanted to ask about your morphine book. Not tonight's. Last Thursday."

"Last Thursday I was on a late. Do you want to sit?" She pushed the second chair out with her foot. Marguerite sat. On the monitor behind the desk a green line moved across the screen in its small, unconcerned way, and from somewhere along the corridor a patient coughed, and another patient, in answer, turned over.

"The ampoules we gave on Thursday," said the Sister. "I'll fetch the book, but I know what it says. Mr. Bellamy in Four had two for his hip, and the woman in Nine, the lady who fell, had one in the afternoon and one at night. That's four. Then the Friday ones. Your count is out."

"How do you know it is my count?"

"Because you came upstairs at one in the morning and you are holding your own elbows." Sister Okafor took her glasses off and let them fall on their cord. "It's no use me pretending I haven't seen you do it before. The last time was the year your mother was ill."

Marguerite let go of her elbows. She laid her hands flat on her knees instead, which was no better. "Two ampoules," she said. "On paper there are two more than in the cupboard. I've checked the wholesaler's note, and the receipt entry, and the ward books, and nothing agrees with anything except itself."

"Which is how you know it is a clerical matter."

"Or how I know it is not."

The Sister considered this, and whatever she thought, she kept to herself for a moment, as one keeps a coin in a closed hand. Then she stood and went into the little office behind the station and came back with the ward's controlled drugs book, a tall volume with a green spine, and she opened it on the desk and turned it to face the pharmacist.

They read it together, with the patience of two women who had both been trained never to assume. The Thursday entries were in the Sister's own round script and in the script of a young staff nurse called Imogen Pryce, with the double signature that the rules required. Mr. Bellamy's two doses were there. The woman in Nine had one in the afternoon and one at night. Nothing was missing from the ward's side. But on the Thursday evening, a little after six, there was a line in a hand that Marguerite did not know, with a pharmacy stock transfer reference, and the words *returned unused, 2 amp*, and two signatures: one a scrawl, and one the careful capitals of someone who wrote slowly in a language not his first.

"Who is this?" she said.

The Sister leaned in. For a long moment her face did nothing at all. "That is Hamish Brennan from the stores. And that is Imogen. They were doing a return, because Mr. Bellamy's dose was changed at the last minute, and two ampoules had been drawn up for the rota and weren't wanted."

"They went back to the pharmacy."

"They'd have gone back to the pharmacy."

"They never arrived," said Marguerite, very softly, and as she said it she felt the thing she had been waiting for since Sunday, which was not relief, and was not alarm, but something nearer to the sensation of a held breath finally leaving the body. The two ampoules had not been stolen from the cupboard. They had been taken out of it, in the ordinary way of business, to a ward, and then they had been taken out of the ward, in the ordinary way of business, and somewhere between the fourth floor and the Cellar they had stopped existing on paper. It was almost worse, she thought, than a thief. A thief at least had a motive.

"Where would he have put them?" the Sister asked.

"He'd have put them on the lift, with a note, and the lift is the one the visitor was talking about," said Marguerite. "The lift that stops between three and two."
