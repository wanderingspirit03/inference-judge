This is the beginning of a short story I've been writing for a regional writing competition (the limit is 6,000 words and I've used about 2,400). Please continue it at length, in the same voice and tense, taking it through the middle and to an ending, and keep the details I've set up (the keys, the stairwell light, the residents) so they pay off. I'd like it to stay quiet and a bit funny, nothing melodramatic.

THE KEYS TO NUMBER NINE

1.

Marguerite Fenwick-Allsop had worked for Hollin Court Housing Association for twenty-three years, and in all that time she had never once been afraid of a stairwell. She had been afraid of other things: of the regional director, who had a way of saying "just a quick one" before a meeting that was never quick; of the photocopier on the second floor, which jammed whenever she was in a hurry; and, on one memorable occasion, of a goose in the courtyard of the Alder Street block, which had taken against her coat. But stairwells she regarded as honest places. They went up, they went down, they smelled of whatever the people on each landing had cooked for dinner, and there was always someone's pushchair in the way.

So she was a little surprised, on the Tuesday in the third week of November, to find that she was standing at the foot of the north stairwell of Hollin Court with her hand on the banister and no real wish to go up.

It was ten minutes past four and already nearly dark. The light on the first landing was out. It had been out since the end of October, and there was a form about it, filled in by Mr Ignatius Pell from flat six and countersigned by three other residents, which she had sent to Maintenance on the fourth of November and which had come back on the eleventh with a stamp on it saying RECEIVED. This was the sort of thing that happened to forms. They were received, and then they were kept, like pressed flowers.

"It's only a light," she said, aloud, to nobody.

"It's only a light," agreed a voice from above, and a small torch came on and swung down the stairs towards her. Behind it was Mr Pell himself, in a long green anorak, holding the handrail with one hand and a plastic bag of what looked like turnips with the other. He was seventy-nine and had lived in Hollin Court since it was built. He regarded the building as a sort of ship, of which he was the first mate.

"I thought you'd come at four," he said. "It's gone ten past."

"I was held up by the Alder Street goose."

"Is it still there?"

"It's dead, Mr Pell. It was dead in the spring. I was held up by the memory of it."

Mr Pell considered this, and seemed to find it reasonable. He came down the last few steps and stood in front of her, a head shorter, with the torch pointed politely at the floor. "It isn't the light I wanted to show you," he said. "It's number nine."

2.

Number nine was on the second floor, at the end of the corridor, next to the fire door that never closed properly. It had been empty for eleven weeks. Its previous tenant, a retired piano tuner called Lazlo Brandt-Okafor, had gone into a care home in September with two suitcases and a cardboard box of sheet music, and had told everybody on the corridor, one by one, that he would be back by Christmas. Marguerite had not wanted to argue, and neither had anyone else. His daughter had signed the paperwork to end the tenancy, quite properly, on the twenty-eighth of September, and the keys had been handed in at the office. Marguerite knew this because she had put them in the key safe herself, in the grey metal cabinet by her desk, on a hook with a label in her own handwriting: NINE, HOLLIN CT, BRANDT-OKAFOR, RETURNED 28/9.

"I went past on Sunday," Mr Pell said, as they climbed. "And there was a smell of toast."

"Toast."

"Toast. And on Monday the corridor was warm. Not the radiators, they've been off on that side since the boiler man came. The door was warm."

"The door."

"I put my hand on it." He demonstrated, stopping on the landing and laying his palm against the air. "Like that. It was warm like a loaf of bread."

Marguerite had a small private rule, which she had developed over the years and never explained to anyone, which was that when a resident described a phenomenon she did not understand, she should not say what it was until she had looked at it. The alternative was to have two theories, hers and theirs, and to lose a great deal of time deciding which was right. Looking was quicker. So she followed Mr Pell up the second flight, where the light also did not work, and along the corridor, where the carpet gave a faint sigh underfoot, and stopped in front of the door of number nine.

It was a perfectly ordinary door. It was painted the municipal blue that the Association had bought in bulk in 2009, there was a brass numeral nine slightly crooked on it, and under the numeral someone had stuck a small yellow note which said, in capital letters, NO CIRCULARS PLEASE. That had been Lazlo's, of course. It had been there for years. She put her hand against the wood.

It was not warm.

"Well," said Mr Pell, a little deflated. "It was."

"It may have been the sun."

"On the north side?"

"It may have been a very determined sun." But she was already thinking about the other thing, which was that on the door frame, level with the lock, there was a thin bright scratch in the paint, the kind that a key makes when it is not quite aimed.

3.

She did not go in. That was one of the things she was proud of afterwards, when she told the story to her sister over the phone, and to the man at the garage, and eventually to the whole of the Thursday coffee morning. The Association's procedure for a void property (she was very clear about this) was that the housing officer might inspect it in the presence of a second member of staff, and that nobody, absolutely nobody, might enter it alone, because of an unpleasant incident in 2016 involving a wasp nest and a locum.

So she did the sensible thing. She went back downstairs, past the broken light, said goodnight to Mr Pell, who gave her a turnip for the road, and walked across the courtyard to the office, where the key safe was.

The office at Hollin Court was a converted ground-floor flat with a counter in what had been the living room and a kettle in what had been the airing cupboard. It was staffed from nine to four on weekdays by Marguerite, and on Tuesdays and Thursdays by an assistant called Bartholomew Osei-Lindqvist, who was twenty-four, wore a different pair of unusual socks every day, and had been with the Association for fourteen months, a record for the post. He was at his desk when she came in, eating a sandwich over a keyboard, which she had asked him not to do.

"Bartholomew. Number nine."

"Number nine," he said, swallowing, and then, with a look that she would later recognise as the look of a young man who had been hoping not to be asked, "what about it?"

"Where are the keys?"

"In the safe."

"Could you check?"

He went to the grey cabinet. He opened it. She watched his back, in a jumper with a hole in the elbow, and she saw it change; the shoulders went up, very slightly, in the way of a man who is counting hooks.

"Hook fourteen," he said. "It's, um. There's a label. But there's no."

"No keys."

"There's a key ring, but there's nothing on it. It's just the ring."

She came round the counter. It was true. The label said NINE, HOLLIN CT, BRANDT-OKAFOR, RETURNED 28/9 in her own handwriting, and the little steel ring hung beneath it, empty and a little bent, like the ear of a dog that has had a long day.

"When did you last see them?" she asked.

"I haven't ever seen them. I mean, I've seen the label." Bartholomew took a breath. "I did sign out a set on Friday, but that was for the Colne Street flat, and that's hook fifteen, and I might have, I mean it's possible that I took the wrong ring and put the Colne Street one back on fourteen and then, then the one I have here"

He stopped. He put his hand in his trouser pocket, a movement of such conscious innocence that Marguerite almost felt sorry for him, and brought out a ring of three keys on a plastic tag. The tag said COLNE ST 4.

"So those are Colne Street," she said.

"Yes."

"And where are the Colne Street keys?"

"On hook fifteen," said Bartholomew, with sudden, shining hope. They both turned to look. Hook fifteen, according to its label, belonged to FLAT 3, ALDER ST. It was holding a lanyard from a funeral directors' conference.

4.

It took them until a quarter to six to establish the facts, which were as follows. The Hollin Court office had four key safes, of which the grey cabinet was the only one used day to day; the others were in the cellar, under the stairs, in a cupboard that had been locked since before Bartholomew's time. The grey cabinet had sixty hooks and fifty-one labelled sets. Of the fifty-one, forty-eight were present and correct. The missing three were number nine, Hollin Court; the Colne Street flat, which Bartholomew was carrying around like a talisman; and, to Marguerite's mild alarm, the master set, which opened the fire doors, the plant room and the roof.

"The master set," said Bartholomew. "That's, that's the one thing you're not supposed to"

"Yes."

"Who has access to the office?"

She thought about it. "Me. You. The regional director, once a quarter, to say it looks tidy. The cleaner on Wednesdays. The man who checks the extinguishers. Mr Pell has the spare office key, on account of the thing with the boiler last winter." She paused, and the pause was a long one. "And anyone who has ever borrowed a pen from the counter while the kettle was on."

Outside it had begun to rain, softly and persistently, in the way of November rain that has no particular argument with anyone. The courtyard lamp came on. In its yellow circle she could see the bicycle shed, and the bins, and the edge of the north stairwell, and above it, on the second floor, the row of windows that belonged to the corridor of number nine. All dark but one. She counted the windows twice to be sure. The last one, at the end, next to the fire door, had a light on, a low and amber light, like a lamp in a window at the end of a street.

"Bartholomew," she said, "would you mind very much putting your coat on?"

"We're not going in," he said. "Are we? You said nobody goes in alone."

"I said nobody goes in alone." She was already reaching for her own coat, and the torch from the drawer. "We are two people, and Mr Pell makes three. I think it is the sort of evening that calls for a second member of staff, and a turnip."

5.

Mr Pell was waiting at the foot of the stairs when they came back across the courtyard, as though he had never gone home at all. He had taken off the green anorak and put on a cardigan, and he had brought a second torch, larger than the first, and a walking stick that Marguerite had never seen him use.

"Window," he said, pointing upwards.

"We saw."

"It's been on since about half past three. I thought, she'll want to see that."

"You might have said, Mr Pell."

"I did say. I said toast." He looked, for the first time that afternoon, faintly wounded, and she was sorry. Behind him, in the dark of the stairwell, the broken light on the first landing gave a small electric tick, as if it were thinking of recovering, and then thought better of it.

They went up in a line: Mr Pell first, because he knew where the loose tread was; Bartholomew in the middle, holding the Colne Street keys in front of him like a hymn book; Marguerite last, with her phone in her pocket and her thumb, for no reason she could have defended, resting on the number of the regional director. The stairwell smelled of damp coats and somebody's cabbage. On the second floor the corridor lay ahead of them, long and carpeted, with the fire door at the end propped open an inch by a folded leaflet. There was, she noticed, a line of amber at the bottom of the door of number nine.

"Warm," whispered Mr Pell, as they came level with it.

It was not warm. She put the back of her hand against the blue paint to be sure, and it was cool and slightly gritty, like any door. But there was a sound from behind it, very low, which none of them had heard from the stairs. It was one note, held, then another, a little higher, and a third. It took her a moment to place, because it was nothing she had heard in twenty-three years of void inspections, and then Mr Pell said, in the voice of a man naming a bird, "That's a tuning fork."

"It can't be."

"Lazlo had one. He used to strike it on his knee." Mr Pell's face in the torchlight had gone soft and stubborn at once. "He said he could never sleep in a place where the room was out of tune."

Marguerite looked at the scratch on the door frame and the empty ring on hook fourteen, and at the yellow note that said NO CIRCULARS PLEASE. She thought of the care home on the Sallow Road, which she had visited once, and of a man who had told every person on the corridor that he would be home for Christmas. She raised her hand, and found, to her slight annoyance, that it was not quite steady.

"Mr Osei-Lindqvist," she said, "you have the Colne Street keys."

"Yes."

"They won't open this door."

"No."

"Then I think," said Marguerite, "that we had better knock."
