Here are the first four chapters of a short story I am writing, set at a small observatory on a hill. I am stuck on what comes next. Please continue the story at length, in the same voice and tense, for at least two more chapters, keeping the characters and details consistent and moving toward an ending that feels earned.

THE LAST CLEAR NIGHT AT HOLLOWAY HILL

1.

The road to the observatory had not been mended in eleven years, and Marit Oyelaran had learned to drive it the way one learns to walk with a stone in a shoe: without thinking, and with a certain private resentment. She took the third bend slowly, where the ditch had taken a bite out of the verge, and the little grey hatchback shuddered and carried on. At the top, the trees gave way. The dome stood against a sky the colour of dishwater, round and white and slightly dingy, like an egg someone had forgotten in a drawer.

She parked beside the other car, an old estate with a cracked wing mirror held on by tape, and sat for a minute with the engine ticking. It was the first of October. The letter from the university was in her bag, folded in four, and she had read it so many times that the creases had begun to turn soft. She did not take it out. She knew what it said. The Holloway Hill Observatory, after one hundred and four years, would be closed at the end of the year, and its telescope, a fourteen-inch reflector that had been the pride of a long-dead Victorian brewer, would be offered to a museum. The staff, which was to say Marit and one other person, would be thanked.

She had been the observatory's assistant director for six years. Before that she had been a graduate student with an unfinished thesis on variable stars and a strong feeling that real life would begin when she stopped being asked questions she could not answer. She had come to Holloway Hill for what was supposed to be a summer.

The door to the main building was unlocked. It was always unlocked. Inside, the corridor smelled as it always did, of cold radiators, floor polish and old paper, with something faintly sweet under it, like pipe smoke from a decade ago that had never entirely left. A radio was playing somewhere, very quietly. Marit followed it to the kitchen and found Osmund Pettigrew standing at the stove with his back to her, stirring a pan of something that could not decide whether it was porridge or soup.

"You are early," he said, without turning round.

"It is ten past four in the afternoon."

"For you, that is early." He turned. He was seventy-three, tall and stooped, with a long nose and pale eyes that were always a little wet from the cold, and he wore the same green cardigan he had worn on her first day. He had been the director since before she was born. "Tonight will be clear," he said. "I have been watching the high cloud since noon, and it is thinning from the west. I think we will have the first clear night in nineteen days."

Marit put her bag on the table. She thought of the letter. She thought, with the tired cunning of someone who has decided not to say a thing, that it could wait until after the clear night.

"What shall we do with it?" she asked.

Osmund smiled, and his whole face changed. "I thought," he said, "we might look at something we have not looked at for a very long time."

2.

The dome was cold in the way that only large, empty, round rooms are cold: a patient and formal cold, the kind that does not rush at you but waits. They went up the iron stairs at half past six, with flasks and blankets, and Osmund unlocked the shutter with a key the size of a spoon. The slit opened with a groan, and the last of the daylight slid across the floor and went out. The sky above them was already deepening, a hard blue turning toward black, and the first stars came out in no particular order.

The telescope stood in the centre of the floor like a sleeping animal. It was a long black tube on a cast-iron mount, its brass fittings worn pale where generations of hands had held them. The brewer, Ambrose Tilling, had paid for it in 1922 and had been buried, according to local rumour, with a star chart folded in his coat. Marit had never found the grave. She had looked.

"Do you remember," Osmund said, as he checked the clock drive, "the first thing you ever showed me through this tube?"

"Albireo," she said. "You said it was the most honest double star in the sky, because one is gold and one is blue and neither pretends to be something else."

"I said no such thing. I would never say anything so sentimental."

"You said it twice."

He grunted, which was his way of conceding. He was bent over the eyepiece rack now, choosing, and she watched his hands. They were large hands, spotted, with a slight tremor that had not been there when she arrived. He had told her that it was the cold, and she had decided to believe him.

By eight, the sky was entirely dark, and clearer than she could remember. The Milky Way came up over the eastern trees like smoke from a distant fire. Marit slewed the telescope to the Andromeda galaxy, a smear of pale light, and then to the Double Cluster, and then, because it was there and because it was the sort of thing they did, to the Ring Nebula in Lyra, a tiny smoke ring hanging in black space. She found herself speaking quietly, as one does in a church, though no one was listening but the old man. When she looked up from the eyepiece, he was sitting on the observing stool with his hands between his knees and his face turned toward the slit.

"What is it?" she asked.

"Nothing," he said. "I was thinking about the year I came here. Nineteen seventy-nine. We had a comet that spring. I stood out here for six nights and I never once felt cold." He laughed at himself. "Forgive me. It is the kind of night that makes old men talkative."

She wanted to tell him then. The words were ready in her mouth, but he had begun to talk about the comet, and the way the tail had looked like a long, pale finger pointing out of the world, and she could not interrupt. At half past ten, they went down for tea. At eleven, the cloud began to come back from the south-west, thin and silver at first, and then not thin.

"Well," Osmund said, as the last stars went. "That is that. Nineteen days of cloud for three and a half hours of sky. I have been well paid."

3.

The letter came to light the following morning, and not in the way Marit had planned.

She had come down to the kitchen to find Osmund at the table, in the green cardigan, with a cup of tea going cold in front of him and an envelope beside it. It was not her envelope. It was long and cream-coloured, with the university's crest in the corner, and it had been opened with a knife, neatly, along the top. For a moment she thought it was another copy of her own. Then she saw that it was addressed to him alone, and that there was a second page, folded separately, that she had not been sent.

"You knew," Osmund said. He did not look up.

"I was going to tell you last night."

"I think you were going to tell me for about a month." There was no anger in his voice. That was the worst of it. "It is all right. They wrote to me in August. I have known since then."

Marit sat down across from him. The kitchen clock, which ran six minutes fast, ticked on the wall. Outside, the weather had turned: a low grey rain was falling, steady and unpromising, over the lawn where the old transit instrument stood under its tarpaulin.

"August," she said. "You said nothing."

"I wanted one more clear night." He turned the cup in its saucer. "That was all I wanted. I thought, if the autumn gives me one, I will tell her afterwards, and we will be sorry together. And the autumn gave me one. Last night. So that is done." He smiled, a little crookedly. "I did not quite plan how to be sorry."

She noticed the second page then, and reached for it, and he let her. It was a short, formal note from the dean's office. It did not concern the closure. It said that the Tilling bequest, the small endowment that had paid for the dome's upkeep since 1931, was to be wound up, and that a sum of forty-one thousand pounds would be released to the observatory "for such final purposes as the director may reasonably determine, provided these are completed before the thirty-first of December."

"Final purposes," Marit said.

"Yes."

"What does that mean? What can you do with forty-one thousand pounds?"

"I have been asking myself since August." He leaned back. The chair creaked. "I could repair the roof, which leaks. I could send the plate archive to the library, where it will sit in a basement. I could buy a very handsome plaque." He paused. "Or I could do something I have wanted to do for thirty years, which is to finish the survey."

She knew about the survey, though not well. It lived in a cabinet in the east room: sixty-three notebooks in Osmund's small, upright handwriting, begun in 1996 and abandoned, as far as she could tell, around 2011. It was a visual record of a patch of sky in the constellation Cygnus, two hundred and twelve variable stars, each one checked by eye on every clear night, week after week, year after year. It was the sort of project that nobody asked for and nobody would ever read. When she had once asked him why he stopped, he had changed the subject.

"The notebooks are not finished," she said carefully.

"No. There are gaps. Some years the weather was unkind and some years I was." He did not explain the last part. "Fourteen of the stars were never closed out. Their periods are long, you see. Some would take another winter or two, if they are to be understood at all. And I do not have another winter or two, Marit, the observatory does not, but the stars do not know that."

The rain strengthened against the window. She listened to it, and to the clock, and to the faint steady ticking of a house that had been loved for a century and would be empty by Christmas. She was aware of a decision approaching her from a long way off, as one watches a figure come up a road.

"There are eleven weeks," she said slowly.

"Eleven weeks and two days."

"At this time of year, in this country, we will get perhaps ten clear nights. Maybe fewer."

"I know."

"And you would need every one of them."

He looked at her then, directly, and for the first time since she had known him, he looked old: not frail, but unguarded, like a house with the shutters taken down. "I would not ask it of you," he said. "It is not your survey. You have a career to think about, and an offer from the institute in the spring, which I know about, because Pauline at the village post office cannot keep a secret." The ghost of a smile. "I am only telling you what I am going to do, so that you will not be surprised when you find me on the lawn at three in the morning in a blanket."

Marit picked up the cold cup of tea and drank it, all of it, to give herself something to do. Then she stood, went to the cabinet in the east room, and took down the first of the sixty-three notebooks.

4.

The notebooks smelled of pencil and damp. She carried the first six to the kitchen table and opened them one by one under the lamp, while Osmund made a second pot of tea and pretended not to watch her. The early pages were beautiful. Each night had its own heading: the date, the time, the transparency of the sky graded from one to five, and then the long columns of magnitudes, estimated by eye against a ladder of comparison stars. Beside each number was a small mark, a tick or a dash, in a code she did not yet understand.

"The dash means I could not see it," Osmund said, setting down the cups. "The tick means I could, and the circle means I wasn't sure. Do not trust the circles. Most of the circles are me being kind to myself."

By noon the rain had settled in as if it meant to stay. They worked through the kitchen's slow afternoon, the lamp on in the middle of the day, and Marit began to see the shape of the thing. The survey was not a catalogue. It was a long conversation with two hundred and twelve stars, most of which had answered faithfully, rising and falling in their small private rhythms, and fourteen of which had gone quiet, or strange, or too slow to follow. Next to the entry for a star in Cygnus labelled only by a number, in a margin note dated March 2009, she found a line that was not about magnitudes at all.

She read it twice. Osmund was at the sink with his back to her.

"It says," she said, "'M. asked tonight whether the star would still be here when we are not. Told her yes. Did not say how many of them are not.'"

The tap ran. After a moment he turned it off, and dried his hands on a tea towel, thoroughly, one finger at a time.

"My wife," he said. "Margarethe. She used to come up in the winters and hold the torch. She was a terrible observer. She talked through everything, and she could never remember which way was north." He folded the towel and hung it on the rail. "She died in the spring of 2011. After that I did not feel like writing down numbers for a while, and then a while was a long time."

Marit did not say she was sorry. She had learned, in six years at Holloway Hill, that Osmund Pettigrew could be thanked or argued with but not consoled, and that the kindest thing was to turn the page. She turned the page.

"Then these are the fourteen," she said, and began to list them on the back of an envelope: their numbers, their last recorded magnitudes, the dates they had gone silent. By four o'clock she had a table. By five, she had worked out that nine of the fourteen could be closed out in a single winter if the weather gave them eight clear nights, and that the other five would need a second season, which they did not have, or a different method, which they might.

"A different method?" Osmund said.

"The camera," she said. "The one in the cupboard. The one you said you did not trust."

"I do not trust it."

"I know. But it would count photons on the five faint ones while you did the other nine by eye, in the old way, and if we are very careful we could tie the two scales together on the nights they overlap." She heard her own voice quicken and felt the old thesis come back into her hands, the pleasure of a problem that had edges. "It would not be the survey you started. It would be a survey with a seam in it."

Osmund considered this for a long time. Outside, the rain was easing, and a thin, cold light was coming back into the garden, and the tarpaulin over the transit instrument was shining like the back of a seal.

"A seam," he said at last, "is better than a hole. I have always said so." He had never said so. She let it go. "Very well. We shall begin tonight, if there is any sky at all, and if there is none, we shall begin tomorrow."

She looked at the sheet of figures on the table. There were eleven weeks and two days. The offer from the institute would wait until spring, or it would not. For the first time since she had come up the road on the first of October, with a letter in her bag she could not bring herself to mention, Marit Oyelaran felt neither dread nor resentment. She felt something quieter and more difficult to name: the feeling of being, however briefly, exactly where she meant to be.
