THE LAST DRAWER

1.

The workshop on Fennel Lane smelled of beeswax and cold iron, and had done so for sixty-one years. Ottoline Marsh unlocked it at ten to seven, as she had every working morning since she was seventeen, and stood a moment inside the door while her eyes got used to the grey. The long benches were where they always were. The band saw squatted under its dust sheet.

She put the kettle on the ring and counted the hours of work left in the book. It did not take long. A pair of oak benches for a village hall, due in March. A child's chair, with a carved fox on the back, paid for by a grandmother who kept telephoning to ask after the fox. And the walnut dresser for the Aldous house, which was the only large job and the one that kept her awake.

2.

Pell came in at half past seven, shaking rain from his coat like a dog. He was twenty-three and had been with the workshop for two years, and she had stopped expecting him to leave. He hung his coat on the nail by the glue pot and said, without being asked, that the dresser panels had finished drying overnight and looked all right.

"Looked all right," Ottoline said. "Or are all right?"

"Are, I think." He rubbed his nose. "There's a little bow in the left door. About the width of a coin. I wasn't going to say until you'd seen it."

She went to look. The panel lay on the bench where he had clamped it, its figure dark and rich, as handsome a piece of walnut as she had ever planed. And across it, if she held her eye low, ran a long shallow curve that no customer would forgive. She laid her palm against the wood as if it were a horse that had gone lame.

"Wet board," she said. "My fault. I should have sticked it longer."

"It was Mr. Dunmore's timber."

"It was my signature on the cheque." She straightened. "We will cut it again. Same figure, if there is any left."

There was not enough left. They both knew it, and neither said so.

3.

The post came at eleven. Between a seed catalogue and a flyer lay a thick cream envelope with the Aldous crest on the flap, and Ottoline carried it to the finishing room and opened it with a chisel. The letter was brief. Mrs. Aldous regretted that the family's plans had changed, that the new kitchen would be fitted by a firm in the city, and that she wished to know the sum owed for work already done so the matter could be closed. She hoped Ottoline would understand. She signed it with her first name, which she had never done before.

Ottoline read it twice and then put it in the drawer of the finishing bench, the last drawer, the one that stuck. It was where the firm's bad news had always gone.

She opened the drawer wider than it wanted and looked at what it already held. The tax notice. Two unanswered letters from the bank. A photograph of the workshop in 1964, the men in it long dead. She put the Aldous letter on top of the pile and shut the drawer with her hip.

Pell was in the doorway. He had the careful face of someone who has heard a page turn in another room.

"Tea's brewed," he said.

"Thank you."

"If it was bad, you needn't tell me." He looked at his boots. "But I'd rather know before the others do. Somebody ought to tell Dev and Marguerite before they hear it in the pub."

Ottoline looked at him for a long while. Out in the yard, the rain had slackened to a drip from the gutter, steady as a clock, and for the first time that year she thought she might not be able to keep the doors open past Easter.

"Come and sit down, Pell," she said. "I'll tell you now. Then we'll go and tell them together."
