I'd like you to carry on this story for me, at length, in the same dry and affectionate voice. Write the next two or three scenes. In them the night shift should discover what is wrong with the big press, and Wilhelmina should have to decide whether to tell her brother. Keep the tone and the details consistent with what I've written.

THE NIGHT SHIFT AT MERRIWETHER AND SONS

I.

Merriwether and Sons, Printers, had been printing the Tarnbury Gazette since before the railway came, and the men who ran the big press believed that the paper would not appear if they stopped believing in it. This was a harmless superstition, and like most harmless superstitions it cost the firm a fortune in candles, lucky bolts and tea.

Wilhelmina Merriwether had inherited the business at thirty-four, together with her brother Augustin, who had inherited the debts. They shared the office on the first floor above the composing room. She kept the books and he kept an optimistic mood, and between them they had maintained the arrangement for eleven years without either of them mentioning which of the two jobs was harder.

On the night this story begins, a Thursday in late November, the Gazette was due to carry the full report of the county agricultural show, the results of the council by-election and a notice of sale for the Fox and Compass, which the brewery had finally given up trying to run. It was a thick issue. It needed the big press, which was called Hortensia, for reasons nobody remembered.

II.

At ten past eleven Hortensia coughed, shuddered, and produced a sheet in which every letter e had been replaced by a smudge.

Old Barnaby Tuck, the head pressman, was the first to reach it. He had spent forty-one years with the machine and spoke to it in a low, reasonable voice, the way a man speaks to a horse that has stopped at a gate. He stopped the rollers, wiped his hands on a rag, and took off his cap, which was how the other men knew it was serious.

"Not the ink," he said.

"Not the paper," said Dorrit, the apprentice, who was seventeen and had learned from Barnaby that you could eliminate a fault by naming everything else.

"Not the plates. I set them myself, Thursday afternoon, with the whole shop watching." Barnaby bent to look under the cylinder with a lantern. He stayed there a long time. When he straightened his face had gone the colour of putty.

"It is the e," he said, quietly. "The letter itself. Somebody has been at the type."

They carried the forme to the stone and looked at it under the lamp. Every lower-case e in the whole issue, two thousand and some, had been filed down by a hair, so slightly that no eye would find it until the ink went on. It was done carefully, by someone who knew the trade, and who had known exactly which case to open.

Dorrit whistled. Barnaby told him not to.

III.

Wilhelmina was asleep in the office armchair, in her coat, when Dorrit woke her. She listened to the whole account without a word, then asked what time the first newsagent's van left.

"Five," said Barnaby.

"Then we have six hours, a spare font in the cupboard, and an apprentice who whistles." She rubbed her eyes. "Barnaby, who has the key to the type cupboard?"

He did not answer at once, and she understood from the silence that the answer was a short list, and that her brother's name was on it.
