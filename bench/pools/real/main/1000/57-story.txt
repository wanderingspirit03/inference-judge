**The Hum**

**1.**

The clinic on Parry Street opened at nine, but Edda Voss was always there by half past seven. She liked the hour before the others came, when the waiting room smelled of floor polish and the radiator ticked as it warmed. She turned on the lamps one by one, wiped the glass of the reception counter, and set out the small bowl of mints that no patient ever took.

In the back room, she lined up the tools on a clean cloth: the tiny screwdrivers, the tweezers, the suction pen, the wax guards in their paper strips. Thirty-one years she had been doing this. When she started, a hearing aid was a beige thing the size of a plum, and people hid it under their hair. Now they came in with devices the size of a grain of rice and asked whether they could have them in a colour.

Her first appointment was at nine fifteen. The name in the book was Mr. A. Lindqvist, new patient, referred by his doctor. No other notes.

**2.**

He arrived at nine sharp, which she took as a bad sign, since people who are early are usually frightened. He was tall, about seventy-five, in a good grey coat buttoned wrongly by one hole. He stood in the doorway and looked around the room as if checking for exits.

"You are Mrs. Voss," he said, very loudly.

"Edda," she said, and she turned her face so that he could see her lips. "Please sit. You can speak quietly here. It is a small room."

He sat, and the chair creaked under his hands. He told her that his wife thought he was deaf. He told her that he did not agree. He had a perfectly good ear, he said, and the whole trouble was that people mumbled, and that the television had got worse, and that the church was built badly. Edda nodded and wrote nothing down yet. She had learned that the first ten minutes of a new patient were not for notes.

"When did you last hear a bird?" she asked.

He looked at her for a long time. "Last spring," he said. "I think. There was a blackbird on the fence. Or my wife said there was."

She took him into the booth and put the headphones on him, and she watched his face while the tones came, one by one, from low to high. At the low tones he pressed the button at once, almost eagerly. At the high ones, nothing, nothing, and then a tiny movement of his thumb that he seemed to regret. When she took the headphones off him, the sound of the room came back to her as if from underwater.

"It is not a bad result," she said, because it was true, and because he needed to hear it first. "But you have lost the high sounds, and those are the ones that carry consonants. That is why people seem to mumble."

He did not answer. He was staring at the little graph on her screen, the line that fell away to the right like a cliff. His hands, she saw, were shaking very slightly.

"Mr. Lindqvist," she said. "Is there something you hear that other people do not?"
