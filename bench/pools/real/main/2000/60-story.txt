Below is the start of a short story I'm working on, narrated by a junior tax adviser. Please continue it for at least three more chapters in the same first-person voice and dry tone, keeping Mr. Penhallow's habits and the mystery of the receipts consistent. I'd like the story to head towards something surprising, but not a cheap twist, and please keep the office setting central.

THE SHOEBOX

1.

In my first week at Lindqvist and Oyelaran Tax Advisers, Mrs. Oyelaran told me that every client lies, but only the interesting ones lie about the wrong thing. I wrote this on a sticky note and put it on my monitor, where it stayed for eleven months, until a cleaner threw it away along with a half-eaten sandwich I had also been meaning to deal with.

I was twenty-four. I had a degree in accounting, a second-hand suit that had belonged to my uncle, and a desk by the radiator in an office above a shop that sold only umbrellas. The shop was called Rain Check. In all the time I worked there I never saw a customer go in. Mrs. Oyelaran said this meant they were laundering money. Mr. Lindqvist, who was eighty and mostly asleep, said it meant they were very good at umbrellas.

Our busiest season was January, when the self-employed brought us their lives in carrier bags, and our quietest was August, when we alphabetised the filing cabinets and argued about the kettle. It was a Tuesday in late October, which has no season, when Mr. Penhallow came in.

I knew who he was before he sat down, because Mrs. Oyelaran had warned me about him the week before in the tone she used for weather forecasts. Ambrose Penhallow was seventy-one, a retired clockmaker, and a client of the firm for thirty-four years. He had never missed a deadline. He had never claimed a single expense that was not supported by a receipt. And he had never, ever, explained anything.

"Miss Adeyemi," he said, lowering himself into the chair opposite mine as if it might object. "I understand that I'm yours now."

"Mrs. Oyelaran has asked me to look after your return this year," I said.

"Yes. I'm afraid it's a bit larger than usual." He lifted a shoebox onto my desk. It was a man's size eleven, brown, with a lid tied on by what looked like a bootlace. It made a sound as it landed, a soft, dry, papery sound, like a very large bird shifting its feet.

2.

I should explain about the receipts.

In thirty-four years Mr. Penhallow had sent us one folder a year, a grey folder with an elastic strap, and inside it, sorted by month and clipped in bundles, every receipt for every purchase that touched his business. Screws. Brass blanks. Mainsprings, jewelling oil, magnifying lenses, a replacement kettle for the workshop, train fares to a trade fair in Leipzig. The folders were famous in the firm. Mr. Lindqvist used to take them out at the Christmas lunch and read the totals aloud as if they were poetry.

This was not that kind of delivery. I untied the bootlace and lifted the lid, and found some two hundred slips of paper, none of them clipped, none of them sorted, and all of them for the same amount.

Four pounds twenty.

I turned over the first one. It was a till receipt from a café on Quillon Street, dated the fourth of March last year, and it listed one pot of tea for one and a toasted teacake. The second was from the same café, dated the sixth. The third, the ninth. I ran my thumb through the pile and saw the same name printed along the top of every slip, Brannock's Tea Room, in a typeface so cheerful that it felt rude.

"These are all from one place," I said.

"Yes."

"On, I think, every few days for the past year and a half."

"It's two hundred and eleven visits, to be accurate. Tuesdays and Fridays, and the odd Saturday when it rained." He folded his hands. "I should like to claim them."

"As a business expense?"

"As a business expense."

I thought of the sticky note on my monitor. "Mr. Penhallow, I'd need to know what the business was. Tea and a teacake is a personal cost unless there was a client meeting, or a working lunch with a supplier, or a reason connected to the trade. Your workshop closed in March last year, if I'm reading the file correctly."

"The workshop closed. The business did not."

He said it with such calm that I wrote it down. Then I looked at what I had written and felt, for the first time that week, the particular itch of a person who has been handed a question when she was expecting a sum.

I tried once more. I asked whether anyone had met him at the tea room, whether he had taken notes, whether a customer or a supplier or a former apprentice had sat opposite him. He listened to each question with real courtesy and answered none of them. When I ran out, he stood, buttoned his coat from the bottom up, the way men of his generation do, and rested one hand on the shoebox as if it were a sleeping dog.

"Four pounds twenty," he said, "is the price of a pot of tea for one and a toasted teacake. It is also, if you divide it carefully, the price of something else. I would rather you found that out yourself, Miss Adeyemi. Mrs. Oyelaran says you are the sort who does."

Then he went down the stairs, past the umbrellas, and out into the rain without taking one.

3.

I did what any sensible junior would do, which was to take the shoebox to Mrs. Oyelaran and say nothing for forty seconds.

She looked at the receipts. She turned a few of them over. She picked one up by its corner and held it to the window, as if it might have a watermark, and then she put it back and said, "How many?"

"Two hundred and eleven. I counted them twice."

"What's the date range?"

"March last year to last Friday."

"And the amounts?"

"All four pounds twenty."

"Not a penny different?"

"Not one."

She was silent, and the radiator ticked. Through the floor I could hear the faint, gentle, unvisited quiet of Rain Check.

"Tea rooms vary their prices," she said at last. "They put them up in January. They have specials. A person who goes to the same café two hundred times does not spend exactly four pounds twenty each time, unless he orders exactly the same thing, in exactly the same way, and the café does not change its prices for a year and a half, or unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the receipts are not from the café." She laid a finger on the printed name. "Ask yourself who prints a receipt that cheerful."

I took the lid, the bootlace and the box back to my desk and sat there until the radiator had finished ticking. On the edge of the lid, in pencil, in the small upright hand of a man who had spent his working life engraving numbers on the backs of watches, was a single line I had not seen. It read: *Ask Brannock, not the bank.*

I rang the number on the slip. It was answered on the first ring by a woman who said, "Brannock's," in the tone of someone who had been waiting for the call for a year and a half.
