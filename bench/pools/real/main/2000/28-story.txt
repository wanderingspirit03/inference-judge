THE WELL AT HOLLOWFIELD

1.

The bus left them at the end of the lane, where the tarmac gave up and became chalk, and the driver did not wait to see them off. Marit stood with her rucksack between her feet and watched the tail lights shrink down the valley until the hedges swallowed them. Behind her, eleven other students were making the small noises of people who had been told the place would be rustic and had not believed it.

"Is that it?" said a boy called Jonas, pointing across the field at a long low barn with a corrugated roof and a single chimney. Beside it stood a row of white tents, flapping slightly, and a portable toilet block that had been painted, at some point, with a mural of sunflowers.

"That's the school," said a woman's voice, and they turned. She had come up the lane from the other side, on foot, in boots that had once been yellow. She was perhaps sixty, wiry, with grey hair cut short as if she had done it herself in a car mirror. "I'm Dr Ilse Brandvold. You can call me Ilse, since you'll be seeing me at six in the morning with no coffee in me and I'd rather not be formal. Welcome to the Hollowfield Field School. Six weeks, one trench, no mercy."

She did not smile when she said it, but Marit thought something at the corner of her mouth considered the idea.

They were given tea in tin mugs in the barn, which smelled of woodsmoke and damp canvas, and a long table was cleared of trowels and sieves so that they could sit. On the wall behind Ilse hung a plan of the site drawn in blue and red ink: a large rectangle for the farmhouse that had stood here until the 1890s, a smaller one for the dairy, and in the lower left corner, a circle drawn with a compass and labelled in careful capitals, WELL? The question mark had been gone over so many times that it had torn the paper.

"Before anyone asks," Ilse said, "yes, we think there's a well. No, we have not found it. The tithe map of 1841 shows one in the yard. The 1890s survey shows nothing. A farmer who lived here told my predecessor in 1974 that his grandfather filled it in with the cellar stone from the old chapel, and my predecessor wrote that down and then died, and since then every field school student has dug in a different place and found a different set of nothing."

Somebody laughed. Ilse let them.

"This year we have a new idea," she went on. "Dr Okonkwo-Reyes from the geophysics department has been out with a resistivity meter and says there is something round and soft under the old yard. Round and soft is promising. It might be the well. It might also be a buried tractor tyre. Your job is to find out which, without breaking anything, and to write down everything you do while you do it."

Marit was a second-year, doing the school because her department required it, and because her mother thought fresh air would cure whatever it was that had kept her indoors all of last winter. She had not told anyone that she was a little frightened of soil. Not of dirt, exactly, but of the way it kept things. She had read once that a grave in clay could preserve a body for centuries, and the idea had stayed with her like a coin in her shoe.

2.

On the second morning they were given their trench. It lay thirty metres from the barn, a rectangle of four by three marked out with string and pegs by someone who had clearly been fussy about it. Ilse paired them off. Marit got Jonas, who had talked all through breakfast about a sailing trip he had done, and a supervisor called Wen, a doctoral student with a shaved head and a laminated checklist, who spoke to them in the careful voice of someone who has repeated a speech many times.

"First thing: you don't dig, you scrape," Wen said. "Trowel flat, pull towards you, thin layers. If you can see a colour change, you stop and tell me. If you find anything bigger than your thumbnail, you stop and tell me. If you find something you think you should not touch, you definitely stop and tell me."

"What if it's very shiny?" Jonas asked.

"Then you really stop."

The topsoil came off in a quarter of an hour, dark and crumbly, full of roots and the white threads of fungus. Beneath it, the colour changed to a mottled yellow-brown, and the first thing Marit found was a fragment of blue-and-white pottery no bigger than a postage stamp. She held it up between two fingers like something that might bite.

"Willow pattern," Wen said, without looking. "Victorian. Bag it, label it, put the context number on the tag. Context 104. Write it in your notebook as well, in pencil. Ink runs."

By lunchtime they had filled three finds bags with clay pipe stems, nails, and a button made of bone. By mid-afternoon the sun had come out over the ridge, and Marit had stopped being afraid of the soil, because it was simply hard work and her knees hurt.

It was Jonas who noticed the stones. He had moved to the north corner to clear a patch around one of the pegs, and he sat back on his heels and said, in a voice with no joke in it, "Marit, come and look at this."

A line of flat stones, fitted edge to edge, curved out of the north wall of the trench. They were small, the size of loaves, and set with a precision that did not look like a farmyard. Wen came across, crouched, and ran a finger along the top of the nearest one without touching it.

"That's a curve," she said quietly. "A proper one. Don't clean it. Don't brush it. I'm getting Ilse."

While they waited, Marit noticed that the soil beside the stones had a different smell. It was not the sweetness of rot, nor the dry mineral smell of the chalk. It was cold and metallic, like the inside of a tap that has not been used for years, and it rose from the gap between two stones in a thin breath that she felt on her wrist.

She did not say anything. She told herself that it was only air moving through a loose joint. But when Ilse arrived, jogging across the field with her yellow boots slapping and Dr Okonkwo-Reyes behind her carrying a tablet, Marit saw the older woman stop dead at the edge of the trench, look down at the stones, and then look, very quickly, at the barn, and at the date on the plan pinned inside it, as if she were counting something.

"Everyone out of the trench," Ilse said. "Please. Calmly. And nobody goes near it tonight."
