Here are the first three chapters of a story I'm writing. Please carry on from where it stops and write at least the next two chapters, at proper length, in the same voice and with the same tense. Keep the characters consistent, let the tension about the missing container build, and don't wrap it up too early.

THE LAST BERTH

1.

At Harrow Quay the cranes never really stopped, they only changed their minds. Anselm Voss had been listening to them for twenty-six years from the glass box of the gate office, and he could tell from the pitch of the whine whether a driver was new, whether the wind had turned, whether it was going to rain. Tonight the sound was wrong. It was the long, patient drone of a crane holding something up and waiting to be told where to put it.

He put his tea down and looked at the screen. Berth Four, the Calloway Star, a feeder ship out of the northern ports, discharging since six. The yard plan showed every box in its place. The yard plan, as Anselm had learned long ago, showed the world as the planners wished it to be.

"You're doing the face," said Perpetua, without looking up from the stack of seal logs.

"I have no face."

"You have the face you get when a number does not match a number." She was nineteen, three months into the job, and she had a way of speaking to him as if he were a slightly dim relative who ran the family shop. He liked it. Nobody else at the port had talked to him like that since his wife died.

"Berth Four says it has landed three hundred and twelve boxes," he said. "The gate system says three hundred and eleven have been given a yard slot."

"So one is in the air."

"Maybe."

He pulled on his orange jacket and his hat and went out into the wet. The quay smelled of diesel, brine and hot brakes. Rows of containers rose around him, red and blue and a faded green, each stamped with the name of some company in some country he would never visit. The straddle carriers moved between them with their legs apart like patient herons. A driver he knew, Teodoro, lifted two fingers from the wheel in greeting.

Anselm walked the length of the discharge lane and counted aloud, as he always did, under his breath. At the end he found Callum Rae, the night supervisor, standing with a radio against his chest and the sort of expression that made Anselm's stomach go quiet.

"Don't," Callum said.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said it with your whole body. It's a reefer. One reefer, forty foot, off the Calloway Star, bay twelve. The crane driver swears he set it down on the chassis and the truck driver swears he never got it. The tag in the system is live. It's still talking to us. It says minus eighteen inside and it says it's moving."

"Moving where?"

"That's what I would like to know."

2.

The container was called HMLU 4410862 and it had been loaded in a town Anselm could not pronounce, with a cargo that the manifest described only as frozen goods, chilled, sensitive. Nobody at the terminal paid much attention to a manifest. A manifest was a wish a shipping line made on the customer's behalf.

By midnight they had walked every row in the reefer stack and checked the sockets, the plugs and the alarms. The box was not in any of them. Callum had turned his radio to the open channel, and for an hour the port crackled with voices asking the same question in different accents: has anyone seen a forty-foot reefer, grey, line colours blue and white, number ending eight-six-two.

Perpetua found the first clue, which annoyed Anselm greatly because he had walked past it twice. In the seal log from the afternoon shift, a line had been written in a hand that was neither careful nor hurried, the hand of someone writing with their mind on something else. It recorded a reefer leaving by Gate Two at 17:42 under the name of a haulier called Pellam and Daughters, eight hours before the Calloway Star had even tied up.

"That cannot be right," Anselm said. "The ship wasn't here."

"The number is the same, though." She tapped it with a pencil. "Eight-six-two. Look. And the seal number is one digit different from the one on the ship's papers. Four-four-seven-one-nine against four-four-seven-one-six."

"A typing mistake."

"Or someone sent a truck for a box before the box arrived."

He felt, then, the thing he had spent twenty-six years learning not to feel at work, a quiet cold interest, like a man who has spotted a coin in a drain. He did not say it aloud. He asked instead for the camera footage of Gate Two between five and six, and Callum, with the reluctance of a man whose supervisor will hear about this in the morning, unlocked the room at the back where the old recorders were kept.

The recorders hummed. The picture on the monitor was grey and flickering, and in the corner of every frame the time ran forward in small white digits. At 17:38 a white truck pulled up to the barrier. It had no company name on the door, only a strip of tape where a name had been. The driver wore a cap pulled low. He did not look at the camera, which in itself was unusual, because everyone looked at the camera, if only to curse it.

"I know that truck," said Teodoro, who had come in dripping and stood behind them with his hands in his pockets. "Or I know the way it sits. It leans to the left. Front spring is gone."

"Pellam and Daughters have six trucks," Callum said. "None of them leans."

"Then it's not one of theirs."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Outside, a crane lowered something heavy onto steel, and the whole building trembled very slightly, as if it too were waiting to be told what had happened.

Anselm looked at the clock. It was ten past one. The Calloway Star was due to sail on the morning tide, and in six hours a customs officer named Ilse Brandvold would arrive to sign off the discharge, and she would count, as she always counted, three hundred and twelve boxes.

3.

Harrow Quay at two in the morning belonged to the people who could not sleep and the machines that did not need to. Anselm and Perpetua walked the perimeter fence with a torch, because the camera on the north side had been out since the storm in March and nobody had yet decided whose budget it belonged to.

"Tell me again why we're doing this ourselves," Perpetua said. "There are police. There is a security firm with a van."

"The security firm sends a man called Dennis, who sits in the van and listens to football. And the police will ask for a report, and the report will say a gate clerk wrote the wrong time in a log." He shone the torch along the base of the fence.

They found the gap behind the old cold store, where the fence met the wall of a building that had been abandoned since the fruit trade moved to the southern terminal. The wire had been cut cleanly and then pulled back into place, so that from ten paces it looked whole. Beyond it, in the mud, there were two parallel grooves, deep and curved, the track of something heavy being dragged on a low trailer.

"They didn't take it out through the gate at all," said Perpetua slowly. "The gate log is a decoy. Somebody wanted us to look at Gate Two."

"Or somebody wanted a record that said it left, so that no one would ever look for it inside."

He crouched and put his glove into the groove. The mud was still soft. Not hours old, then. Minutes, or an hour at most. He had the strange conviction, which he would remember later and be embarrassed by, that the container was very close and listening to him breathe.

From the dark behind the cold store came a low, steady hum. It was not a crane. It was the sound a refrigeration unit makes when it has been running for a long time and is beginning to struggle.

Perpetua took out her phone and switched on its light, and Anselm put his hand over it. "Not yet," he said. "Whoever cut that fence is not far away, and a reefer on battery is a reefer with a deadline. If the cold fails, whatever is inside will start to tell its own story."

"What is inside?"

He thought of the manifest, with its three vague words, frozen goods, chilled, sensitive, and of how the word sensitive had no business sitting on a manifest at all.

"Somebody who paid a great deal of money for it not to be asked," he said.
