Could you go through it section by section, summarising each in a few sentences, and then give me a list of recommendations for the trustees with the reasoning behind each? HIGHCRAG MOUNTAIN RESCUE TEAM
Annual Operations Report, 1 October 2025 to 30 September 2026
Prepared by Rosalind Achterberg (Team Leader) and Gethin Mortlock (Treasurer)

1. Summary of the year

Highcrag Mountain Rescue Team was called out 94 times this year, against 81 in the previous year, an increase of 16 percent. The team has 38 active members, down from 41, of whom 24 are fully operational on the hill, 9 are trainees and 5 are support members who work in the base and the control vehicle. Callouts used 2,310 volunteer hours on the hill and in the base. Eleven casualties were airlifted by the coastguard helicopter, and the team assisted the ambulance service on nine occasions with stretcher carries from places where a vehicle could not reach. Three casualties died before or during the rescue, a year in which the team was also asked to recover one body after a search of two days. Income was 148,200 pounds and expenditure 128,400 pounds, leaving a surplus of 19,800 pounds.

The 94 callouts are broken down below by the activity of the casualty or subject.

Callouts were busiest in July and August with 14 each, and quietest in February with 3. Forty-one callouts, or 44 percent, began between 17:00 and midnight, and 19 callouts needed teams to stay out through the night. The median time from the page to the first team member on the hill was 38 minutes, against 41 last year. The median time from the page to reaching the casualty was 1 hour 52 minutes. In 12 callouts the casualty could not be located for more than three hours because the location they gave was wrong or their phone had no signal.

Causes. Slips, trips and falls accounted for 39 callouts. Navigation errors and being benighted accounted for 23. Equipment failure, such as a broken bike frame or a dead headtorch, was behind 7. The remaining 25 were medical conditions, missing persons and other events.

3. Casualties and outcomes

Of the 94 callouts, 66 casualties were treated and walked or carried to a roadhead, 11 were airlifted, 9 were handed to the ambulance service on the hill, 3 died, 3 were found safe and well and needed no treatment, and 2 callouts were stood down before the team reached the location. Our casualty carers gave oxygen on 14 occasions and used the team's vacuum mattresses 21 times. The average age of casualties was 41, but 17 casualties were under 25 and 12 were over 65.

4. Membership and training

The team lost six members during the year, mostly because of moving away for work, and welcomed three new trainees. Recruitment events in March and September brought 63 applicants, of which 20 attended the assessment weekend and 9 were accepted. Fully operational members are expected to complete at least 20 training evenings and days a year. Average attendance this year was 17, and 8 operational members did not meet the target. The team also ran a three-day casualty care course in May for 14 members, a swiftwater awareness day in June, and a joint search exercise with two neighbouring teams in October. Two members completed the national leadership course and two qualified as search managers.

Concerns. The age profile of the team is rising: 15 of the 24 operational members are over 45. Only five of the 38 members are women. The new trainees all live within 20 minutes of the base, which is good for response, but the team still has no operational members in the western valley, from which 17 of the 94 callouts originated.

5. Vehicles and equipment

The team runs three vehicles: the control vehicle, a 2016 long-wheelbase four-by-four ambulance conversion, and a 2011 minibus. The control vehicle failed its MOT in January and was off the road for 19 days at a cost of 4,300 pounds, during which the team borrowed a vehicle from a neighbour. A new four-by-four, bought with a grant and a donation, entered service in June at a cost of 36,900 pounds, which includes fitting out. The older ambulance conversion has now been sold for 5,200 pounds. A new set of 24 radios replaced the old handsets at a cost of 11,800 pounds. Spending on personal clothing and technical equipment was 29,500 pounds. A drone with a thermal camera was received as a gift in March and has been used on 7 callouts, finding the casualty on 3. Two team members are trained to fly it, and the team has not yet written a policy on its use at night.

The surplus of 19,800 pounds has been added to reserves, which now stand at 96,500 pounds, or about 9 months of expenditure at the current rate. The trustees' policy is to hold at least 12 months. Legacy income fell by 11,000 pounds compared with the previous year, and one grant of 15,000 pounds, which accounted for 10 percent of income, ends next March and cannot be renewed.

7. Safety and learning

There were four team member injuries, all minor, and one near miss in which a loose rock fall in a gully narrowly missed a stretcher party. The review recommended that spotters be posted on all gully carries. Two incidents of fatigue were reported after the long overnight search for the missing person in November. The team has drafted a policy that no member should be on the hill for more than 14 hours without a relief, but it has not yet been adopted.

8. Priorities for next year

The leader proposes to recruit in the western valley, set up a second equipment store there, write the drone policy, adopt the fatigue policy, replace the minibus, and find replacement income of at least 15,000 pounds before March.
