I wrote the opening of a short story and I'm stuck on how it continues. Please carry it on at length in the same voice, in the same quiet, close third person, for roughly the next two chapters. Keep Ines's habits and the building as they are, and let the green volume matter.

# The Reading Room

## One

By the third week of October the scanner in the basement of the Calloway Museum had been running for nineteen months, and Ines Varga had stopped hearing it. It made a sound like a long, patient breath: a bar of white light travelling under glass, a pause, a click, the next page. She had learned to tell the days apart by what the sound did. On good days it kept an even rhythm. On bad days the click came late, and she would look up from her screen to find that the operator, a young man named Tobiah who wore the same green cardigan every day, had stopped to wipe the glass with the cloth he kept in his pocket.

There were four of them in the digitisation team, which was a grand name for a long room with no windows. Ines checked the images. Tobiah scanned. Mrs. Adeyemi-Lowe catalogued, typing each description into the database in a voice she kept low, as if the objects could hear. And Corwin, who was the team's manager and also its only person with a title, spent most of his time upstairs explaining to the trustees why the work was taking longer than the grant had promised.

The grant had promised the whole of the Fennimore Collection by the following June. That was eleven thousand letters, ledgers, maps and photographs, gathered over a hundred and forty years by the museum's founding family and its friends, and kept in grey boxes on steel shelves that smelled of dust and cold tea. As of Monday they had finished four thousand two hundred.

Ines's job was to be the last pair of eyes. A scan came up on her screen, a pale rectangle on a darker field, and she checked it against a short list: edges all in frame, colour card visible, no thumb, no shadow, no fold hiding a line of text. If the image passed, she pressed the space bar and it went on to the archive. If it failed, she pressed another key and the page went back to Tobiah. She did this about three hundred times a day, and she had found that the only way to do it well was to pretend that every page mattered equally. The moment she began to sort them into the interesting and the dull, her attention slipped and the thumbs got through.

She was good at it. That was what Corwin had said when he hired her, in his rushed, warm way, as though he had just discovered a fact about her. She had not told him that she was good at it because for most of her life nobody had looked at what she did, and she had learned to look very closely at her own work in case someone one day did.

She kept her tea in a blue tin mug on the left side of her desk, and she moved it to the right whenever she wanted to think. Mrs. Adeyemi-Lowe had noticed this in the first month and never mentioned it. It was one of the small kindnesses of the room.

## Two

On the Thursday, a box arrived that was not on the list.

It was Box 61, which should have contained the correspondence of a Miss Hester Fennimore, 1911 to 1923, but when Tobiah lifted the lid he stopped and said, "Ines, I think someone has packed this wrong."

Inside, under a layer of tissue paper that had gone the colour of weak tea, lay a single bound volume with a cloth cover the green of old pond water. There was no title on the spine. Tobiah opened it with the flat of his hand, as they had all been taught, and showed her the first page. It was a drawing of a room: a long table, six chairs, a window with the blind half drawn, and in the corner a small cabinet whose door was open. Under it, in pencil, someone had written, "The Reading Room, as it was on the day we stopped."

Ines looked at the date on the box label, then at the list pinned beside the scanner. Box 61 was meant to hold letters. This was not a letter. It was not in the database, and when she searched the Fennimore index for the words "reading room" she found nothing, not even a stray reference, though she knew that the old house on Marlowe Street, which had been given to the museum in 1952, had once had a library on the ground floor.

"Do we scan it?" said Tobiah.

"We tell Mrs. Adeyemi-Lowe," said Ines. "She decides what it is. Then we scan it."

But Mrs. Adeyemi-Lowe was at the dentist, and Corwin was upstairs with the trustees, and the volume sat on the cradle under the glass with the lights of the scanner off, like a patient waiting. At four o'clock Tobiah went home. At half past, Ines was alone in the room with the hum of the dehumidifier, and she found that she had turned the second page.

It was a list. Six names, written in a careful, sloping hand, each followed by a date and a single word. Hester, 1911, Flowers. Amos, 1913, Tea. Lucan, 1914, Windows. There were three more, and the last of them was crossed through with one straight line, so neatly that it might have been done with a ruler. Under the list the same hand had written: "Whoever keeps the room keeps the key. Whoever keeps the key must tell no one but the next."

Ines read it twice. She was aware of her own pulse in a way she had not been since the winter she left her last job. It was only an old book. Houses like Marlowe Street were full of such things: club rules, recipes for ink, lists of who was to bring the sandwiches. She told herself this clearly and in order, as she told herself the checklist for every scan, and none of it quite settled.

She turned the third page and found a photograph tipped in at the corners, a small square print gone brown. It showed the long table from the drawing, the six chairs, the half-drawn blind. Four people sat at the table with their hands flat in front of them, and nobody was looking at the camera. They were looking at the open door of the cabinet in the corner. Someone had written on the white margin below, very small: "Before."

She put the volume back on the cradle exactly as it had been. She turned off the lamp. She stood in the doorway for a moment, and in the dark the scanner seemed, for the first time, to be breathing out of time with her. Then she took her blue mug from the right side of the desk, rinsed it at the little sink, and left it upside down on the rack, which she never did, because she meant to come back in the morning before anyone else.
